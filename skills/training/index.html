The one idea to teach

Everything in this training hangs on one sentence. Say it early, draw it, and come back to it in Module 7.

Your database doesn't know how to rate a sales call. Neither does your automation. An AI step only knows what you send it on that run. The rubric has to travel with every transcript.

There are three separate jobs, and people blur them together:

JobWhat it doesExamples
The brainKnows how to judge a call: the rubric, the rules, the feedback formatThe skill (for chatting with Claude); the automation prompt (for unattended runs)
The plumbingMoves the recording and transcript from place to place and calls the AIZapier, Make, n8n, a script
The memoryStores each call, its score and its review, so you can sort, filter and spot patternsNotion, Airtable, a spreadsheet, a CRM

The highlighted box is the only place judgment happens. Everything else just moves files.

1 · Start with the end

Show what a finished review looks like before explaining anything, so every later step has a reason.

On screen
One reviewed call in your database: the score, outcome and flags, then the review on the page: the turning point, keep three, fix three, the drill.
Say
"Every call my team takes lands here, scored, with the exact line where the deal was won or lost and one drill for the next call. Nobody typed this. By the end you'll know how to build it, and the one piece most people get wrong."
Do
  • Point at one quote with a timestamp. Say: every claim is backed by a line from the call.
  • Sort the database by score to show it's a ranking, not just notes.
Check
Ask: "What would you do with this every Monday?" Get two answers before moving on.
0:05–0:15

2 · What a great skill looks like

Learners can name the parts of a strong skill and why each part exists.

On screen
A well-built example skill: its main SKILL.md and its references/ folder.
Say
"A skill is a set of instructions plus reference material that Claude loads when the task comes up. Before I wrote a word of mine, I took apart skills that already worked and copied their structure, not their content."
Teach

The anatomy, in order:

  1. Description: when to use it. This is how the AI decides to load it, so list the real phrases people will type ("score this call," "why didn't this close").
  2. Cardinal rules: two or three rules it never breaks. For this skill: no transcript, no verdict; objections are symptoms; coach the rep, not the call.
  3. Calibration: what it asks on first use (the offer, the price, the best call on file).
  4. The process: numbered steps in a fixed order.
  5. Output format: exactly what every answer contains.
  6. Compounding: how it improves: logs, corrections, saved examples.
  7. Failure modes: the specific ways it goes wrong, so it can avoid them.
  8. References: the deep material, kept out of the main file so it stays readable.
Watch for
People write a skill as one giant prompt. The main file should be short enough to read in five minutes; depth goes in the reference files.
0:15–0:30

3 · Gather the source material

Learners know where good source material comes from, and why full transcripts beat summaries.

On screen
Left: an AI summary of a closer's video. Right: the real transcript of the same video. Scroll to the same idea in both.
Say
"My first pass used web search, and I got summaries of summaries. Pages failed to load, some were paywalled, and for one closer almost nothing was usable. The skill only got good when I pasted in full transcripts of the actual videos."
Do
  1. Pick four or five experts whose methods you trust. Here: Hormozi (CLOSER), Miner (NEPQ), Shelby Sapp (gap selling and objections), Dan Martell (diagnose, then enroll).
  2. For each, find their longest teaching video on calls or objections, and copy its transcript (most video sites show one under the video).
  3. Add one data source to keep the gurus honest. Here: Gong's research on talk ratios and question counts.
  4. Ask the AI to extract their teaching and tag every idea as verbatim, from a summary, or secondhand.
Teach

Provenance tags matter. Feedback that quotes a closer must quote them accurately. The skill only puts verbatim lines in quotation marks and paraphrases the rest. This stops the AI from making up an authoritative-sounding quote.

Watch for
Don't put whole transcripts inside the skill. Distill them into rules and keep quotes short. It's lighter, it's fairer to the creators, and the skill reads faster.
0:30–0:40

4 · Distill one framework

Turn four experts' methods into one call structure the AI can grade against.

On screen
The master stage table in references/closer-frameworks.md: one row per stage, one column per expert.
Say
"Different names, same skeleton. Every one of them frames the call, digs for pain, paints the outcome, makes waiting feel costly, pitches only what solves the pain, asks, handles concerns, and locks it in."
Teach
  • Map, don't merge. Lay each expert's steps against the same eight stages. Where they line up, you've found a real principle.
  • Find the rules that run through everything. Here there were two: the prospect says the pain, not the rep; and the sale is made in discovery, not at the close.
  • Settle disagreements on purpose. Hormozi says restate the problem back. Miner says repeating everything back sounds scripted. The house rule: restate the one core problem, once, in their words. Write the ruling down so the AI doesn't flip-flop.
Check
Ask learners: "Which two experts would disagree most about how you run your calls?" That disagreement is their first house rule.
0:40–0:55

5 · Make it gradable

Learners can turn "good selling" into a rubric that scores the same way every time.

On screen
references/scorecard.md, then references/transcript-signals.md.
Say
"If you can't write down what a 1, a 3 and a 5 look like, the AI will make it up, differently every time. The rubric is where your judgment becomes repeatable."
Teach
  1. Ten dimensions, weighted to 100. Pain depth and objection handling weigh most, because that's where these calls are won and lost. Weights change by call type (one-call close, discovery, setter).
  2. Anchors for 1, 3 and 5. A 3 is "done, but shallow or in the rep's own words." A 5 is "done fully, in the prospect's words, with evidence."
  3. The evidence rule. No quote, no score. This one rule does more for quality than anything else.
  4. Automatic flags. Things that matter regardless of the total: never asked for the sale, talked right after the price, compliance risk. A great score can't hide them.
  5. Signals to scan for. Phrases visible in a transcript: the rep's filler and hedging, the prospect's "if I could at least…", partner mentions, money worries. A prospect signal the rep ignored is often the turning point of the call.
  6. The diagnosis rule. The objection at minute 42 was usually caused at minute 8. Feedback names the earlier skipped step, not just a better comeback.
Watch for
A rubric that rewards any close. A rep who pressured a bad-fit buyer into a refund should score lower than a rep who correctly said "this isn't for you."
0:55–1:05

6 · Package and test

Install the skill, prove it scores well on calls you already understand, and package it to share.

On screen
Your AI tool's skills settings, then a chat with one test call pasted in.
Do
  1. Zip the skill folder (main file plus references). Some tools want a .skill file, which is the same zip renamed.
  2. Check the description is under the tool's limit. Mine went over 1,024 characters and had to be trimmed.
  3. Upload it, then run your three known calls: the clean close, the loss, the messy follow-up.
  4. Check the ranking matches your gut. If the clean close doesn't score 85+, fix the rubric or the house context before you automate anything.
  5. Check one quote from each review against the transcript. Every quote should actually be there.
Say
"Test on calls you already understand. If it can't tell your best call from your worst, automating it just spreads the mistake faster."
Check
The three test calls rank in the right order, and every quote checks out.
1:05–1:25

7 · Wire the pipeline

Learners understand all three automations, and above all how the database "knows" how to rate a call.

On screen
Automation editor: Zap A, then Zap B, then the review step (Zap C). Keep the database open in another tab and watch a row change as each one runs.
Teach

Zap A, when a recording is ready: trigger on a new cloud recording → save the video to Drive → create a database row with the call name, rep, date, length, meeting ID and recording link. Status: Waiting on transcript.

Zap B, when the transcript is ready: trigger on a new transcript → save it to Drive → find the row by meeting ID → add the transcript link. Status: Ready for review.

Zap C, the review: trigger when status becomes Ready for review → get the transcript's text (not just its link) → send it to the AI model with the review prompt → read the JSON that comes back → write the score, grade, outcome, flags, top fix and drill into the row, and add the review to the page → set status to Reviewed last.

Say
"Here's the part nobody explains. The skill lives inside the AI chat app. An automation doesn't load skills. When Zap C calls the AI, the model knows nothing except the prompt and the transcript we send on that run. So the rubric has to go in the prompt, every single time. That's what the automation prompt file is: the skill, compressed into one prompt, with an output a machine can read."
Do
  1. Open automation/notion-review-prompt.md. Show the three parts: the system prompt (the rubric), the user message (call details plus transcript), and the JSON output.
  2. Fill in the house context block: offers, prices, call type, reps, house rules.
  3. Paste the system prompt into the AI step. Map the transcript text and the row's fields into the user message.
  4. Make the allowed outcome values in the prompt match the database options exactly, down to the dash.
  5. Add a parse-JSON step, then map each field to a database property.
  6. Run it on one test row and watch it fill in.
Watch for
Three common breaks: sending the transcript link instead of its text (the AI can't open your Drive); free-text output that can't be mapped to fields; and setting status to Reviewed before the writes succeed, which hides failures.
Check
Ask: "If I change the rubric tomorrow, where do I change it?" The answer is the prompt in Zap C, with a new version number, not the database.
1:25–1:30

8 · Close the loop

The system gets better every week instead of drifting.

Teach
  • Corrections become house rules. When a manager says "we do that on purpose," add it, dated, to the house rules in the prompt and in the skill.
  • Version the prompt. Every review records its rubric version, so you can compare old and new scores fairly.
  • Outcomes outrank scores. After 20 or more calls, check whether high scores actually close. If a dimension doesn't predict closes, lower its weight.
  • Use the right tool for each job. The automation scores every call. The full skill, in a chat, handles coaching conversations, rankings across reps and drill plans.
On screen
Three database views: Flags this week; Score by rep; Closed with a low score (refund watch).

The review step: two ways to build it

Both work. Teach both, then recommend one based on the audience.

Recommended start

Automation calls the AI

Zap C sends the review prompt and the transcript to the model and writes the JSON back.

  • Runs on every call within minutes, with no one involved
  • Structured fields every time, so sorting and filtering just work
  • You maintain a condensed prompt alongside the full skill
  • Costs one AI call per sales call
Highest quality

An AI agent on a schedule

An AI assistant with the full skill installed and database access runs daily: "Review every row marked Ready for review."

  • Uses the complete skill, including rep patterns and saved examples
  • Can compare against earlier calls from the same rep
  • Batch, not instant; depends on the agent having access to the database and transcripts
  • Needs a strict output template, or the format drifts between calls

The user message Zap C sends

Call name: {{Call name}}
Rep: {{Rep}}
Call date: {{Call date}}
Length (min): {{Length}}

Allowed outcome values (choose exactly one):
Closed – Collective | Closed – Sales Accelerator | Closed – Inner Circle | Follow-up | No decision | Not a fit

Allowed revenue band values (choose one, or null):
$25K–$50K | $50K–$250K | $250K–$1M | $1M+

TRANSCRIPT:
{{full transcript text}}

Swap the outcome and revenue band lists for your own database's options. The full system prompt, JSON format and field mapping are in automation/notion-review-prompt.md.

Troubleshooting

SymptomLikely causeFix
Reviews use a different scorecard each timeThe AI step has no rubric, or a vague onePaste the full system prompt into the AI step
Score is in the page text but the number field is emptyThe output is prose, not JSON, so nothing maps to the fieldRequire JSON output, add a parse step, map each field
Outcome select is blank, or gets a new odd optionThe model's wording doesn't exactly match the select optionsList the exact allowed values in the user message
The review talks about the wrong callThe transcript link was sent instead of its text, so the model guessedAdd a step that reads the document's text and send that

Wrong speaker namesTranscription mislabels speakersPass the rep's name in the user message; the prompt says to fix labels
Very long calls fail or get cut offThe transcript exceeds the step's size limitUse a model with a larger context, or raise the step's limit
Rows stuck on "Ready for review"Zap C errored before the last stepCheck the automation's run history; status should change only after the writes succeed

Homework for learners

  1. Pick three experts and get one full transcript from each.
  2. Fill in the master stage table for your own sales process, including one house rule where two experts disagree.
  3. Write anchors (1, 3, 5) for the three dimensions that matter most in your calls.
  4. Run the review prompt by hand on three calls you know. Adjust until the ranking matches your gut.
  5. Only then build the review step, and test it on one row before switching it on.
