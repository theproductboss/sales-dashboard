# Closer Frameworks: The Source Material

This file distills what elite closers teach about running a sales call, then merges it into one master stage model that the scorecard grades against. Read the master model first. The individual frameworks are there so feedback can say "this is the Hormozi Overview step you skipped" and mean something specific.

## Provenance tags (read before quoting anyone)

Material comes from transcripts and summaries of these people's videos and podcasts, gathered November 2026. The Miner, Martell, and Sapp sections are now built mainly from full video transcripts the user supplied. Every claim carries a tag so the skill never claims more certainty than it has:

- **[V]** verbatim from a transcript of the person speaking (auto-captions, so minor wording errors are possible)
- **[S]** from an AI or editorial summary of their content: the idea is theirs, the wording may not be
- **[P]** third-party paraphrase or secondhand report
- **[G]** general, widely taught version of their method, not tied to one transcript; check it against their own material before quoting it as theirs

When a review cites a closer, only put [V] lines in quotation marks. Everything else gets paraphrased and attributed as "Miner teaches..." and never as a quote. Never invent a quote to make feedback sound authoritative.

## The master stage model (what the scorecard grades)

Every high-ticket call framework below lands on the same skeleton. The names differ; the order doesn't.

| # | Stage | Job of the stage | Hormozi CLOSER | Miner NEPQ | Sapp | Martell |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Frame and connect | State why you're here, set the agenda, get permission to ask questions | (pre-C) | Connecting; get them to qualify to you | Short intro, justify why you're there, rapport after the ask (first 10%) | Control the frame (doctor frame) |
| 2 | Clarify the why | Why they're here, why now | Clarify | Situation; camouflaged "what caused you to..." | Current state: probing questions | Qualify broad to narrow |
| 3 | Dig the pain | What they've tried, why it failed, what it costs, layer by layer | Overview (pain cycle) | Problem awareness, consequence (layer it) | Provoking questions; conversational threading | Direct, specific questions |
| 4 | Paint the outcome | A specific, emotional picture of after, pressure-tested | (C's 12-month question) | Solution awareness; close the "at least" open loops | Desired state; magic wand; why 50K not 49 | Challenge beliefs into the buying zone |
| 5 | Cost of inaction and label | What happens if nothing changes; restate the core problem; get agreement | Label | Consequence; push-away "why now?" | Cost of inaction; get them to say why now | The gap |
| 6 | Pre-handle and pitch | Surface objections before price; present only what solves their stated pain | Sell the vacation | Objection prevention | Pre-empt spouse, price, time; tie the bow; value formula | Pre-handle objections |
| 7 | Ask and handle | Ask clearly, stay silent, handle concerns, re-close | Explain away concerns | Deframe; identity frame | Micro buy-in, price drop, AARR | Make the offer; assume the sale |
| 8 | Lock and reinforce | Payment, next steps, stop buyer's remorse, referrals | Reinforce | | Reality check, future pacing, planned rapport | Takeaway close, book a meeting from a meeting, referral at the aha moment |

Two rules run through all of it:

1. **The prospect says the pain, not the rep.** Value in the buyer's mind is how well their problem was articulated back to them (Hormozi [S]). Sapp's version: the brain won't let a person's own words be a lie, so get them to speak their own sale [V paraphrase].
2. **The sale is made in discovery, not at the close.** Sapp: discovery is 80 percent of the call and where objections get pre-empted [V]. Miner: by the time you ask the closing question, they've already decided; the close is just the next logical step or an objection [V paraphrase]. Martell: pre-handle the objections earlier so they don't show up at the end [V].

**Where the frameworks disagree, and the house ruling.** Hormozi's Label step restates the problem back ("So what I'm hearing is..."). Miner says parroting back everything the prospect says sounds scripted and kills trust [V]. Ruling: restate the one core problem, once, in the prospect's own words, and ask for confirmation. Don't paraphrase every answer.

## Alex Hormozi: CLOSER

Sources: "How I Built A 20+ Sales Team [The Process]" (caption transcript, rosetta.to); "I Taught 115+ Salesmen My Closing Framework" (Ep 305/306, deciphr.ai summary with excerpts); "How to Close a Deal on the Phone Before They Hang Up" (Ep 952, Shortform summary); "How to do a Sales Pitch" (sozai.app transcript).

**The core rule.** Everything in the script is a question. No paragraphs, because reps start reading them. Questions make the prospect pause and answer, which makes the script work for any rep [V paraphrased closely]. Only three things should come out of a closer's mouth: [V: "Questions, restatements, and anecdotal stories."]

**C, Clarify.** [V: "clarify why the person is there."] Example questions [V]: "what made you get on the call?" / "What's your goal?" / "Why is that important to you?" / "What would it look like 12 months from now if this ideal outcome were to be achieved?"

**L, Label.** Restate their problem and get agreement [V: "So what I'm hearing is you've done X, Y, and Z, and the missing link has been X... Is that right?"] Once they confirm, the rep has a problem to solve.

**O, Overview past pain.** [V: "So, what have you done in the past?"] then "Why did that not work for you?" Loop through every past attempt (the "pain cycle") until they run out, then recap the pain.

**S, Sell the vacation.** [V: "sell the vacation, not the plane."] Sell the outcome, not the mechanics. About three short stories, roughly 30 seconds each, one per pillar of the solution. The more the buyer has to do themselves (DIY, done-with-you), the simpler it must feel; done-for-you can show more steps. Too many steps: chunk them into bigger buckets. Ask permission before pitching.

**E, Explain away concerns.** [V: "crazy people buy without obstacles. Normal people have concerns."] Drill objection responses with flashcards until they're automatic. Three buckets [V]: "It's price, it's stall, it's decisionmaker." Price means they don't see the value. Decision-maker: lean on agreements already made. He walks people through yes/no decisions (do you like me, the product, do you think we can get you this outcome, do you have access to the money) then [V: "What card do you want to use?"]

**R, Reinforce.** Added later because the sale continues through the relationship [S]. Right after the close, send something personal (founder video, handwritten card) to stop buyer's remorse.

**On managing and reviewing.** [V: "If you're not recording every single call that your people are on, one, you're not compliant."] In 1:1s, reps bring their best, worst, and average call; the manager asks them to show where they clarified the problem, outlined the pain, asked for the sale, and handled objections. [V: "You can never make a sale if you never ask for it."]

**Mistakes he names:** statements instead of questions; explaining too much; feature stacking; not clarifying pain; never asking for the sale; objections that weren't drilled.

## Jeremy Miner: NEPQ (Neuro-Emotional Persuasion Questioning)

Sources: user-supplied full transcripts of two Miner videos: an objection-handling role play ("How do you get your prospects to persuade themselves?") and "7 hacks to outsell 99% of salespeople." Supplemented by Triple Session session pages [S]. Captions render his name "Miller" in places; it's Miner.

**The philosophy.** The prospect has the problem; the rep has the solution. So the prospect should qualify to the rep, not the other way round [V paraphrase]. Objections aren't pre-planned by the prospect; something the rep said, asked, or how they said it triggered them [V paraphrase]. So the job is objection prevention more than objection handling. [V: "human beings only make decisions emotionally. Then they justify logic."] The two biggest drivers of change are [V] "pain and the fear of future pain." If the prospect doesn't relive the pain and fear the future, [V] "they feel no need to... Change."

**Consequence layering (go six layers deep).** In the role play, he takes "health coaching for entrepreneurs" and keeps asking what happens next: lost productivity → less money → business at risk → out of business → unemployed → can't provide for the family. [V: "See how I could go six layers deeper than that. That's where the sales made. Not up here about getting healthier."] What to grade: did the rep stop at the first, surface-level consequence?

**The deframe (for "now's not a good time").** The prospect's frame is "not now, keep in touch." Instead of rebuttals, ask permission and then a consequence question in a concerned tone [V]: "How can I communicate to you that you might be making a mistake without you getting upset with me?" The concerned tone plants doubt that the rep knows something they don't, and the guard comes down [V paraphrase]. Then loop back through consequences and the spouse's view, and finish with a push-away question.

**The identity frame.** [V: "why not push it down the road like a lot of executives who never end up getting promoted..."] The prospect pushes back against being "that kind of person" and argues for acting now. Related move [V]: "you don't sound like the type of person who wants to get the least things in life. Would I be right?"

**Pre-handling the partner.** Asking how the spouse feels about the problem continuing gets the prospect to say the spouse wants it fixed, which stops a "talk to my partner" objection before it forms [V paraphrase].

**Tonality.** Three failure modes [V]: monotone (sounds like a telemarketer), too excited (sounds like every salesperson), timid or nervous (creates uncertainty). [V: "Human beings do not follow uncertain people."] Target: assertive, calm, relaxed, a little detached. He calls it HILA [V]: "high intent to help... but you have low attachment." A concerned tone on consequence questions; a curious, slightly confused tone on follow-ups and pattern interrupts.

**Open loops and non-committal language.** [V: "The way someone talks to you is also the way they think."] Phrases like "if we could at least...", "we're not doing that bad," "I'd be okay with that," or small goals ("at least scale to 6 million") signal a limiting belief that comes back later as a smokescreen objection ("not a good time," "need to think it over," "I can do this myself"). Close the loop in discovery by challenging the word ("at least") and re-anchoring the goal, which widens the gap.

**Camouflaged questions.** Generic questions ("What are your biggest challenges?", "What keeps you awake at night?") are predictable and get surface answers [V]. Ask the same thing specifically and indirectly. Examples [V]: "So what's caused you to feel like that might not be enough..." / "what was it that you maybe read or saw that... caused you to feel like, hey, maybe I should look into this a bit further?" / "what do you have in place right now to make sure you're able to retire when you actually want?"

**Trigger words.** "Just circling back," "just checking in," "just following up," canned company boilerplate: these put the rep in the salesperson bucket [V paraphrase]. His follow-up framework [V paraphrase]: use their first name (not "is this John?"), say when you last spoke, repeat back the end result they wanted, then a pattern interrupt like [V] "Did you guys give up on scaling the company or what actually happened?"

**Verbal pacing.** Asking questions too fast gets kneejerk, surface answers [V]. Use verbal pauses so the question feels important and gets a deeper answer. Not slow, just thinking-out-loud. Emphasis matters: "Why is *this* so important to you *now* though?" Keep asking why behind the surface reason until you reach the identity driver (his cabinets example: the real reason was childhood embarrassment, not looks).

**Filler words and bridging.** After each answer, average reps say "Okay, okay. Gotcha, gotcha. I'm curious, let me ask you..." or repeat back everything the prospect said [V]. Both sound scripted and show the rep isn't present; they're thinking about the next question instead of listening for red-flag words and tone. Fix: occasional verbal cues while they talk (ah, right, really, okay; roughly every 15 to 20 seconds), then bridge straight into the next question off their last words ("...how long ago did that actually happen though?").

**Qualifying to the prospect (status).** When a lead says "call me back at 7," most reps agree, which puts them in chase mode, lowers their status, and gets ghosted [V paraphrase]. Better: offer your number and ask for their time frame, or have them book a specific slot in business hours.

**Consequence-question tone [S].** Start with a challenging tone, soften to concern at the end so it doesn't feel like an attack.

## Dan Martell: diagnose, then enroll

Sources: user-supplied full transcript of a Martell sales training video (speaker not named in the transcript; content consistent with Martell: selling software since 17, "Laws of Success" sign-off); "How to Make $100K in 6 Weeks With a Productized Service" (danmartell.com, article verbatim). The danmartell.com material is about chat/DM selling; say so when applying it to calls.

**Mistakes he names [V paraphrase unless quoted].** Overpromising when commission isn't tied to retention (HubSpot tied rep pay to post-sale customer happiness to stop overselling). No process, no skills and drills. Pressuring someone who wasn't ready, so they cancel after the call ("I had 17 sales... five of them refunded"). Not doing the takeaway close. Reciting a script instead of having a talk track. Not documenting what the prospect said, so the customer repeats themselves at onboarding. Bad follow-up: [V: "if you say just following up you lost the sale"]. [V: "the fortunes are made in the followup"].

**Qualify.** Decide what's true about your best buyers, then ask questions from broad to narrow that get a yes or no, and let the wrong people go fast. The best qualifying questions also surface pain ("Do you struggle to hit your numbers every month?") [V paraphrase].

**Setter standard.** A five-star prospect [V paraphrase]: has a problem, knows it, wants help, has tried other things, has a budget, is feeling the pain now. Setters log everything and hand it over as a package. Pair setters and closers in pods so closers can give feedback on lead quality.

**Enrolling, not closing: five steps [V].**
1. **Control the frame.** Amateurs get pushed off course by "what's the price?" Use the doctor frame: ask permission to ask questions so you can figure out what's wrong. [V: "prescribing without diagnosis is malpractice"]. Calls that turn into free consulting don't close, because you released the pressure by giving the answers away.
2. **Direct questions.** [V: "the buyer is going to choose if you're an expert based on the quality of your questions"]. Broad, high-level questions don't show expertise.
3. **Challenge beliefs.** Buyers are either overconfident (they could do it themselves) or underconfident (they need to get ready first). Ask questions that bring them back to the middle, the "buying zone."
4. **Pre-handle objections.** [V]: "have you put a budget aside to solve this problem", "have you talked to your wife about purchasing this", "have you already reviewed this with your team", "have you bought software like this before".
5. **Make the offer.** Assume the sale; the energy doesn't change. Never "are you ready to move forward?"; use this-or-that ("Thursday or Friday?", "medium or large?"), which lets them see themselves as a buyer.

**The takeaway close (lock-in) [V paraphrase].** After the yes, recap the three problems you'll solve, then ask: "minus a global financial meltdown, is there any other reason you might not move forward and get on your onboarding call?"

**Book a meeting from a meeting.** Don't just "send a proposal." Ask what they'd want to see in it, handle the objection, ask again, and if a second meeting is truly needed, book it on this call. Then the no-show line [V]: "if for whatever reason something should come up could you do me the honor of texting me beforehand so I can fill that slot".

**Referrals at the aha moment.** After the buyer's first win, ask for one or two people just like them. He claims this alone lifts sales about 30 percent [V, his claim].

**From the DM-selling article [V].** The gap: [V: "The whole point of a sale, it only happens in the gap."] Commitment test: [V: "On a scale of 1 to 10, is this a now thing or a later thing?"] Objection steps: acknowledge ("Makes sense. I would ask the same."), isolate ("Besides that, is there anything else in the way?"), clarify, offer two paths, close with a question. [V: "Silence closes deals."] [V: "A confused mind never buys."]

## Shelby Sapp: logic plus emotion

Sources: user-supplied full transcript of "How to Sell Anything (Full Sales Course)" (about two hours); Big Deal podcast #137 summary [P].

**Why people buy.** People buy when the perceived benefits outweigh the perceived cost in time, money, and energy [V paraphrase]. Sell both halves of the brain. Her story of two reps who lost [V]: "You sold too much with logic and zero emotion and you sold with only emotion and no logic."

**Logic: positive and negative data.** Quantify what they gain (the KPI: leads, pounds, revenue) and what they lose by waiting. Example [V paraphrase]: at $8K a month aiming for $20K, every month of delay costs $12K.

**Emotion: future pacing and sizzle.** [V: "Where would you want to be in six months if everything worked out perfectly?"] Pressure-test the goal [V]: "Why do you want to make 50K? Why not 49? Why not 51?" Feature plus benefit [V]: "They don't buy a Zoom call. They buy the accountability, the energy, and the live coaching..."

**The opening.** Rapport before purpose is wrong; it reads like an old school friend texting before an ask [V paraphrase]. State why you're there first, then build real rapport. Three parts: keep the intro short, justify why you're talking, get the leverage. Delivery: downward inflection, sentences ending as statements, [V: "don't talk in commas. Talk in periods."]

**Call shape: 10 / 80 / 10 [V].** 10 percent intro (guard is up), 80 percent discovery (guard comes down; this is where the sale is made and objections are pre-empted), 10 percent close and objections (guard goes back up at the price). [V: "sales should be 80/20. 80% your client talking, 20% you talking."] [V: "the person who asks the most questions always controls the conversation."]

**Gap selling: three buckets [V paraphrase].** Current state, with probing questions (what's going on, when did it start) then provoking questions that add emotion (why are you unhappy with that, take me through your day). Desired state: the magic wand, take me through that day. Cost of inaction: if nothing changes in six months, where are you? Going stress → hope → stress creates the inner tension that makes real urgency. Fake scarcity ("three spots left") only attracts broke buyers [V paraphrase].

**Discovery moves.**
- **Conversational threading.** Pull many follow-ups from one pain point instead of moving to the next scripted question. [V: "surface level questions get you a surface level buyer."]
- **Gather ammo.** Don't pitch at every pain point; bank it and plug it into the presentation later ("when you mentioned this, this specifically helps...").
- **Listen to understand, not to reply.** Rotating prompts [V paraphrase]: tell me more, can you give me an example, what does that mean specifically, when did that start, who do you think is responsible?
- **Buyer types.** Emotional buyers want to rant; let them, then regain control kindly ("I could talk about that for hours... is it cool if we get back to it?"). Logical buyers: use their name, mirror their last few words, and pause. Ego-driven buyers: tell a story about someone similar and ask if it fits, so they can open up without losing face.

**Pre-empting objections in discovery [V paraphrase].** Read the signals and surface the objection without inventing one. Spouse: ask whether they made a past similar decision alone or with their partner, not "is your husband the decision-maker?" Price: if they live in cost-based thinking, ask whether they're at a place to make a real investment in solving this. Time: if the problem is three years old, ask why they didn't solve it then and why now. Their spoken answer makes a later "I'll think about it" sound unnatural. Warning: don't create objections out of thin air.

**The transition.** Not "now let me show you what we've got." Tie the bow ("if we did A, B, and C, do you think that would help?") and build credibility with a quick similar-client story, then present [V paraphrase]. Presenting is where you prove you were listening.

**The value formula [V].** Outcome × certainty, minus cost × risk. Make the outcome clear and realistic. Don't accept "unicorn land" goals; anchor to what's typical so certainty goes up. Cost is time, money, and energy, not just price. Address risk (reputation, ego, fear) out loud.

**Asking for the sale.** Get a non-money micro buy-in first ("regardless of price, are you at a place where you can dedicate X time to this?"). Then the price: say "investment," remind them what it gets them, use "just," and keep the energy relaxed. Option close (A or B) or statement close. [V: "The first person who speaks after you reveal price loses."]

**Three things happen after the price [V paraphrase].** (1) A yes: go straight to paperwork and don't oversell. (2) A clarifying question: it is not an objection; answer it and return to paperwork. (3) An objection: run AARR.

**AARR: the four-step objection method [V].**
1. **Acknowledge, never agree.** "I hear you." Not "yeah, it is expensive." Frame it as us versus the objection.
2. **Ask.** Get context and isolate. The spouse objection is often a smokescreen for price.
3. **Answer.** Ask permission first ("Can I challenge that?", meaning the thinking, not the person), then reframe: price versus cost; time versus information; certainty versus information; problem versus symptom. Or use an analogy or a similar client's story.
4. **Redirect.** Re-close right away, before the silence lets them retreat to "I'll still think about it." "Which one gets you closer to your goal?"

**Her objection roulette [V paraphrase].** Think about it: you don't need time, you need information, and you have it now. Too expensive: price is what you pay today, cost is what you pay later. Tried before: past attempts don't change the goal. Bad review: one against many successes, then show testimonials. Talk to my spouse: you'll bring them either a problem or a solution. Scared: you can be scared and still decide.

**Solidifying the sale.** The sale is done when they feel good about saying yes [V paraphrase]. Bring them back to reality (this takes work), future-pace the wins, never go silent during paperwork (planned rapport), and pre-empt the bank's fraud-check decline so it doesn't feel like a sign [V paraphrase].

**Mindset and energy.** [V: "sales is 80% the energy that you bring into it and your mindset."] Reward yourself on hard days, not good days; count the nos. [V: "money is a lagging indicator"]. [V: "sales is personal development disguised as a job."]

**Practice and review.** Words first, then delivery. Use AI to review call recordings ("where did I go wrong?") [V paraphrase]. Her call review scorecard rates five areas out of 10: script, tonality, body language, connection or rapport, energy. Get to 8+ on each before live calls [V].

**Ethics.** Manipulation is getting someone to do something good for you and bad for them; persuasion helps them do something that benefits their future self [V paraphrase]. The mirror test, and [V paraphrase] would I sell this to my sister, best friend, or mom?

## Gong: what the data says

Source: Gong Labs, "Mastering the talk-to-listen ratio in sales calls" (2025 analysis of 326K calls of 10+ minutes, plus the 2016 study of 25K calls). Gong sells call-recording software and the data is correlational, B2B-heavy, and averaged across industries. Use it as a benchmark, not a law.

- Average rep talk share is about 60 percent. Won deals average around 57 percent; lost deals around 62 percent. Low performers swing from 54 percent (won) to 64 percent (lost); top performers hold steady.
- 2016 study: the highest-converting calls sat near 43 percent talk, and talking more than 65 percent of the call tracked with lower conversion.
- Questions: won deals averaged 15 to 16 questions per call; lost deals about 20. More isn't better; interrogation hurts. Top reps spread questions through the call; average reps front-load them.
- Long seller monologues show up in lost deals.
- Pause about two seconds after the prospect stops before responding.

For one-call-close consumer offers, Sapp's 80/20 target and Gong's numbers both apply to discovery. Expect higher rep talk share during the pitch. Grade the two segments separately.
